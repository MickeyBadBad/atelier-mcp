# Style: American Classic (美式经典)

> Sources: Mark D. Sikes, *Beautiful: All-American Decorating and Timeless Style* (Rizzoli, 2016), foreword by Nancy Meyers; Mark D. Sikes, *More Beautiful: All-American Decoration* (Rizzoli, 2020); Bunny Williams's published Architectural Digest features (notably the Richmond, Virginia estate with 3north architects); Williams Lawrence (formerly Bunny Williams Interior Design) firm documentation; The Grand Tourist interview "Bunny Williams: American Decorating Dynamo"; PaperCity Magazine, "Hollywood's Blue Man" (Mark D. Sikes); Pottery Barn / Mark D. Sikes published collection notes; AD's July/August 2022 feature on Sikes & Nancy Meyers. Specific named projects cited inline.
> Last updated: 2026-04-29

## Anchor description

American Classic — sometimes called "All-American" or "American Traditional" — is the residential vocabulary of the East Coast coastal-and-country household, popularized at the high end by Sister Parish, Albert Hadley, Bunny Williams, Mario Buatta, and (in its current accessible form) Mark D. Sikes (per The Grand Tourist, "Bunny Williams: American Decorating Dynamo"). It is "modern and unfussy … classic takes on California indoor/outdoor living, with natural fibers and crisp coloration" (per Sikes, *Beautiful*, publisher description, Rizzoli 2016). Its hallmark is the blue-and-white axis layered with natural fiber rugs, painted millwork, slipcovered upholstery, ticking stripes, and an inherited or auction-sourced antique. The Bunny Williams commandment — "I really live in my house. We entertain … you feel that you could flop down on the sofa. It's not too precious — and yet there are good things in there" (per The Grand Tourist interview) — defines the brief: every surface is meant to be used, but every surface is also "good."

Practical rule: pick **white or cream as dominant**, **one** blue (deep indigo *or* robin's-egg, not both at full strength in one room), and **one** garden-green or barn-red accent. Sikes himself organized *Beautiful* by single color stories per room rather than mixing two saturated stories in one space (per Sikes, *Beautiful*, organizational structure: chapters are color stories — "Blue and White Forever," "Timeless Neutrals," "Garden Greens," "Beautiful Brights," "Sun Faded Hues," "Red My Way").

## Material vocabulary

### In

- **Slipcovered upholstery in natural-fiber white linen or cotton duck.** The Sikes / Hamptons / Coastal-American convention; loose-fit slipcovers signal "indoor/outdoor" living and are washable for actual family use (per Sikes, *Beautiful*, Rizzoli 2016 — slipcovers documented across his California and East Coast projects).
- **Wicker, rattan, woven seagrass, sisal, jute.** Sikes' rooms "include … a touch of rattan" almost universally; he favors "rattan and bamboo furniture, or natural grass rugs" (per Mark D. Sikes editorial coverage and Pottery Barn collection notes).
- **Painted millwork — crown moulding, baseboards, panelled wainscot, painted built-in bookcases.** Bunny Williams: "you have to break up a large space to make it intimate, with big mirrors, big moldings, and big furniture" — the millwork *is* the architecture in this style (per AD Virginia estate feature, Bunny Williams quote).
- **Antique mahogany, cherry, walnut — at least one inherited or auction piece per room.** Williams trained 22 years at Parish-Hadley and continues the antique-as-foundation tradition (per Williams Lawrence firm documentation; The Grand Tourist interview).
- **Blue-and-white porcelain — Chinese export, Delft, Staffordshire transferware.** Sikes signature element: "cheerful blue-and-white porcelain" features in nearly every published Sikes interior (per editorial coverage, e.g. PAGODA RED Stories, "Mark D. Sikes: Mastering Blue and White").
- **Ticking stripe, ginghams, batiks, ikat.** Sikes: "fabrics in stripes, batik, and ikat patterns" — the trio shows up across his published rooms and in his MDS Stripes textile collection (per Moda Operandi, "The Life And Style Of Mark D Sikes").
- **Brass — unlacquered, allowed to age.** Standard hardware in the Williams Lawrence vocabulary; reads "old house" rather than new construction.

### Out

- **Hard contemporary minimalism — concrete floor, single-slab marble waterfall, frameless glazing.** This is the gallery / loft register; American Classic explicitly works in the opposite direction (intimacy, layering, scale-broken-down) per Bunny Williams's "break up a large space to make it intimate" rule (per AD Virginia estate feature).
- **Mirror-polished chrome, lacquered modern metals.** Brass is the metal of the style, and it is *unlacquered*; chrome reads mid-century or contemporary, not American Classic.
- **Greige / all-neutral monochrome rooms.** The whole organizational principle of Sikes' *Beautiful* is *color stories* — "Blue and White Forever," "Garden Greens," "Red My Way" (per Sikes, table of contents). An all-greige room is not American Classic; it is Quiet Luxury (see `quiet-luxury.md`).
- **Industrial loft fixtures — exposed conduit, Edison bulbs, factory pendants.** Wrong style universe entirely (see `industrial-loft.md`).
- **Logos and trend prints.** Williams: rooms should "celebrate the personalities and style of their clients" (per Williams Lawrence firm description), not display brand identity.

## Lighting profile

- **Kelvin range: 2700-3000K.** The American Classic published interior reads warm but not orange — soft white incandescent / 2700K LED replacement is the baseline. Sikes' interiors photograph as bright daylight + warm evening lamp light, not candle-warm.
- **Layer mix: ceiling decorative + table lamps + sconces — three layers, not the Scandinavian seven.** Bunny Williams's Virginia estate living room places "very little wall space, so all the seating had to float" with table lamps on every side table and a ceiling chandelier as the social anchor (per AD Virginia estate feature).
- **Hero fixture style — the canonical references:**
  - **Crystal or brass chandelier in dining and entry.** Williams's Virginia entry pairs "an 18th-century gilt-wood mirror" with the chandelier as the room's center (per AD Virginia feature).
  - **Pleated drum or empire-shade table lamps** (often porcelain ginger-jar bases, or tole urn bases). Sikes' editorial rooms place at least two on every console / sofa flank (per Sikes, *Beautiful*, throughout).
  - **Picture lights** above framed art or above a bookcase — the East Coast tell.
  - **Outdoor lanterns at the porch / breezeway** — the "indoor/outdoor" Sikes signature.
- **Candles for entertaining only.** The brief is "we entertain. There's a big table with a bar on it" (per Williams via The Grand Tourist) — candles are dressed for guests, not as a daily aesthetic anchor (contrast with Scandinavian, where candles are baseline; see `scandinavian.md`).

## Camera bias

- **Wide daylight through tall un-curtained or cafe-curtained windows.** Sikes' published rooms photograph with full sash windows visible, often with simple white curtains tied back — no heavy drapery sheers (per Sikes, *Beautiful*, Rizzoli 2016 photography by Amy Neunsinger).
- **Eye height 1.4-1.6m**, the conventional Western seated-conversation height — emphasizes the deep-seat sofa silhouette and the table-lamp horizon.
- **Three-layer depth — hero antique foreground, slipcovered seating midground, painted millwork + blue-and-white display background.** This is the recurring AD/Sikes/Williams editorial composition.
- **Vertical correction on, but tolerate decorative ceiling moments** (a coffered ceiling, an applied medallion under a chandelier, a barrel-vaulted bath ceiling — Williams's master bath at the Virginia estate uses "a Neoclassical painting … on the master bath's barrel-shaped ceiling" as the photographic centerpiece, per AD).

## Prop vocabulary

Build the prop list straight from the published Sikes / Williams editorial inventory:

- **Blue-and-white porcelain — at minimum one ginger jar, one tureen, one stack of cabbage-leaf plates.** Sikes: "a piece of blue and white instantly adds color, pattern and style to a room" (per PAGODA RED Stories, "Mark D. Sikes: Mastering Blue and White").
- **Hydrangeas in blue-and-white porcelain or silver mint-julep cup.** Sikes' Hamptons palette explicitly cites "blue hydrangeas, hurricane lanterns, ticking stripes, and diminutive florals" (per Mark D. Sikes Newsletter, "Fresh Air in the Hamptons," 2025).
- **Stack of hardcover decorating books, monogrammed coasters, silver tray with bar bottles.** Williams: "There's a big table with a bar on it. The drinks are there, the ice bucket" — entertaining is the visible default state (per The Grand Tourist).
- **One inherited or auction-sourced antique chair / chest / mirror.** Williams: "I like the juxtaposition of new and old. I like mixing contemporary art with traditional art" (per The Grand Tourist).
- **Framed botanical, equestrian, or landscape oil — gilt or painted black-and-gold frame.** The Williams/Sikes wall convention; never an unframed canvas.
- **Topiary in a glazed terracotta pot — myrtle, boxwood, or rosemary.** Sikes' Hamptons signature: "myrtle topiaries" listed first among the project's regional motifs (per Mark D. Sikes Newsletter, June 2025).
- **Pleated lampshade — silk or paper, never bare bulb.** A bare bulb instantly breaks the read.

## Common mistakes

1. **Over-monochrome "modern coastal" greige.** Defaulting to all neutrals + a single seagrass rug + one blue cushion is *Coastal* in the contemporary sense, not American Classic. Sikes himself organizes the entire genre by *color stories*, not by tonal restraint (per Sikes, *Beautiful*, Rizzoli 2016 ToC). Fix: commit to one full-strength color story per room and layer the porcelain and stripes accordingly.
2. **Treating blue-and-white as cobalt-only.** Sikes is explicit: "When people think 'blue and white' they think of stark white and cobalt. But I like to play with my blues by adding in tones of robin's egg, mint, turquoise, lilac, etc. That's what gives the palette dimension" (per One Kings Lane interview, "Mark D. Sikes on Style, Stripes, and His Signature Collections"). Fix: render at least three blue values, not one.
3. **No antiques.** Williams's defining move is "the juxtaposition of new and old" (per The Grand Tourist). A scene with all-new furniture from a single retailer reads "showroom," not American Classic. Fix: place at least one obviously older piece — a chest with knocks, a turned-leg chair, a gilt mirror with foxed glass.
4. **Bare windows or roller shades.** The published convention is white linen panels, cafe curtains, or simple Roman shades — never bare glass and never industrial roller blinds. The Sikes Hamptons palette is built around fabric (per Mark D. Sikes Newsletter, June 2025).
5. **Industrial / metal-jewelry overlay.** Chrome legs, exposed black-pipe shelving, or polished steel pendants pull the room toward Industrial Loft (see `industrial-loft.md`). Fix: replace metal jewelry with painted wood + unlacquered brass.
