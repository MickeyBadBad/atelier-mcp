# Style: Japanese Tea Cafe (日式茶咖)

> Sources: Dezeen 25 February 2014 feature on SunnyHills at Minami-Aoyama by Kengo Kuma; Designboom 27 February 2014 on the same project; Kengo Kuma & Associates project page for SunnyHills at Minami-Aoyama; Interior Design magazine "From A Simpler Time: SunnyHills by Kengo Kuma"; Domus 13 January 2014 on SunnyHills "and the matter of business"; Wallpaper* interview with Shinichiro Ogata on Higashiya tearooms; Kinfolk feature on Higashiya Ginza; Thisispaper feature on HIGASHIYA man Marunouchi; La Muse Blue 2018 feature on Shinichiro Ogata / Simplicity / Saboe; Dezeen 19 November 2021 on Fumihiko Sano Studio's Hishiya guesthouse and restaurant in Kyoto; ArchDaily on Japanese Cuisine Tokiwa by Fumihiko Sano Studio; Schemata Architects' Blue Bottle Coffee project pages.
> Last updated: 2026-04-29

## Anchor description

The Japanese tea-cafe (日式茶咖) is the contemporary continuation of the Japanese tea-room (chashitsu) and the wagashi-confectionery shop, adapted to the third-place cafe format pioneered in Tokyo and Kyoto from the early 2000s and now widely replicated in Taipei, Shanghai, and Singapore. Its visual signature pairs **wabi-sabi** restraint (asymmetry, age-tolerance, raw natural materials) with **sukiya-style** carpentry (the rustic-tea-house architectural lineage referenced explicitly by Fumihiko Sano Studio at Tokiwa, where "the interior design is a Sukiya style to reflect the chef's personal fondness of tea ceremonies" — per ArchDaily, *Japanese Cuisine Tokiwa*). The single most-cited reference is Kengo Kuma's SunnyHills at Minami-Aoyama (2013), which uses the traditional **jigoku-gumi** ("interlocking hell") cypress lattice — "without the use of any nail or screw" — to create "a forest within the busy city centre" (per Dezeen 25 February 2014; Kengo Kuma & Associates project page). For a 3D scene this means: hinoki cypress or cedar slat lattice, paper-and-wood shoji surfaces, tatami or cork floor, low table heights (35–40 cm) with floor-cushion seating where possible, low warm 2400–2700K light, and a single-stem ikebana arrangement.

Practical rule for an AI applying this palette: pick **one** dominant timber (hinoki lattice **or** whitewood plaster wall), **one** floor (cork, tatami, or stone), and **one** accent metal/glaze. Mixing hinoki + walnut + tatami + copper + sumi-ink black at full strength reads as Edo theme park; the genre's discipline is restraint.

## Material vocabulary

### In

- **Hinoki cypress slat lattice (jigoku-gumi).** Kuma's SunnyHills uses "over 5000 metres of wooden slats … to create a 3D structure to envelop the external walls and the ceiling," using a jigoku-gumi joint angled at 30/60 degrees rather than the conventional 90 (per Dezeen 25 February 2014; Designboom 27 February 2014). The technique is "traditionally found in the decorative cabinetry for teahouses and temples" (per Dezeen 25 February 2014). Lattice density typically 6 × 6 cm members at 50–80 mm spacing.
- **Whitewashed timber + copper, the Simplicity vocabulary.** HIGASHIYA man Marunouchi is "composed of natural materials like whitewood and copper" — these two materials carry the room (per Thisispaper). Ogata's broader Simplicity studio philosophy: "softly minimalist, crafted, layered in stillness" with "Japanese Wabi-sabi aesthetic's rawness that over time burgeons into the rusting patina of beauty" (per La Muse Blue 2018).
- **Cork tile flooring.** SunnyHills' choice for both retail floors — quiet, warm-toned, age-tolerant (per Dezeen 25 February 2014).
- **Tatami mats — igusa rush over rice-straw or polystyrene core.** Standard 0.91 × 1.82 m or 1.0 × 2.0 m proportion; 5–6 cm thickness.
- **Shoji — paper screens on cedar frames.** Translucent partition with light-filtering function; canonical at the Hishiya guesthouse renovation by Fumihiko Sano Studio (per Dezeen 19 November 2021).
- **Sumi-ink stained timber for column / beam accents.** Sukiya-style detailing as documented at Tokiwa (per ArchDaily, *Japanese Cuisine Tokiwa*).
- **Stone — flamed granite, river-pebble inlay.** Used at engawa thresholds and water-basin (tsukubai) vignettes.
- **Hand-thrown ceramic ware — Karatsu, Bizen, Hagi, Mashiko.** Used as matcha bowl, mizusashi water jar, single-stem vase. Ogata's S[es] tableware line is part of the Simplicity ecosystem (per La Muse Blue 2018).
- **Hand-forged iron kettle (tetsubin) on iro or sunken hearth.** Functional accent with tactile patina.

### Out

- **High-gloss lacquer, mirror-polished marble, glass-and-chrome.** The genre prizes "the rusting patina of beauty" (per La Muse Blue 2018 on Ogata) — polished surfaces read as Western luxury, not tea-room.
- **Heavy ornament — carved dragons, gold leaf, brocade upholstery.** That vocabulary belongs to the Chinese tea-house genre (see `chinese-tea-house.md`), not the Japanese. Kuma's explicit move at SunnyHills was *constructive* ornament (the lattice is structure, not decoration): "wood joints without glues or nails are the essence of Japanese architecture" (per Dezeen 25 February 2014).
- **Bright cool-white LED.** Wabi-sabi restraint requires warm low-Kelvin light to read the patina and shadow; cool light flattens the genre.
- **Loud, bright, large-volume rooms.** SunnyHills Minami-Aoyama is "less than 300 square metres" across three floors and the 2nd-floor cafe is "designed so that you can take in the flavours while admiring the building from within. Be prepared to wait for a table as only a small number of guests are accommodated" (per Kanpai-Japan; Dezeen 25 February 2014). Density is part of the genre — the room must feel held.
- **Western-height dining tables (74 cm) as primary.** Where the genre is being honored, low chabudai tables (35–40 cm) with floor cushions or low bench seating dominate; Western-height seating is a hybrid concession only.
- **Heavy use of dark walnut.** That belongs to speakeasy or Western mid-century. Japanese tea-cafe wood is *pale* (hinoki, sugi cedar, cypress) or sumi-ink black, not warm-walnut brown.

## Lighting profile

- **Kelvin range: 2400–2700K.** Warm but slightly cooler than speakeasy candlelight — the goal is to read the grain of the cypress lattice and the surface of the matcha bowl, not to obscure them in dim pools.
- **Layer mix: filtered daylight + low ambient + per-table accent.** Translate to ambient at 80–150 lx, table-task at 200–300 lx, decorative (paper lanterns, hanging single bulbs) at 30–60 lx.
- **Daylight filtered through lattice or shoji.** SunnyHills' jigoku-gumi cypress lattice acts as a light-filter — the cafe interior is described as a "forest within the busy city centre" and the room's light is patterned by the slats (per Designboom 27 February 2014; Dezeen 25 February 2014). Render with a strong directional sun broken into 6 × 6 cm-slat shadow patterns falling across the cork floor.
- **Paper lantern (chōchin or andon) as decorative accent.** Single fixture per zone, low-glow.
- **Backlit shoji panels.** A panel of paper-and-wood backlit at 2400K reads instantly as Japanese tea-cafe; cross-reference to %Arabica's "backlighting" of white walls (per Travel Caffeine on %Arabica Higashiyama) — the technique is shared but the surface (paper vs plaster) reads the genre differently.
- **Low pendant over the tea counter.** Single hand-blown glass or paper-shade pendant ~80 cm above the counter.

## Camera bias

- **35–50mm equivalent, eye-height seated *or* kneeling (0.7–1.2 m).** The genre's conventional published angle is from the seated guest's POV, not standing — preserves the low ceiling height and the lattice as canopy.
- **Strong directional light + lattice-shadow as compositional element.** Designboom and Dezeen's published photography of SunnyHills consistently uses the cypress-shadow patterning across the cork floor or the pale stair as the foreground texture (per Designboom 27 February 2014; Dezeen 25 February 2014).
- **Foreground prop: single matcha bowl, single dried-branch ikebana, single tetsubin kettle.** One object, centered or rule-of-thirds; the empty space around it is the photograph.
- **Vertical correction on. Do not tilt up.** Tea-room ceilings are deliberately low; tilting up shrinks them further.
- **Cropped detail shot of joinery.** SunnyHills' jigoku-gumi joints are themselves a published subject — Designboom and Dezeen each show macro shots of the joint geometry as standalone images (per Designboom 27 February 2014).

## Prop vocabulary

- **Single-stem ikebana arrangement.** One dried branch (corkscrew willow, cherry, plum) in a hand-thrown vase, asymmetric placement.
- **Matcha bowl (chawan), bamboo whisk (chasen), bamboo scoop (chashaku).** Even when the cafe serves coffee, the wagashi-and-tea ritual set is part of the prop vocabulary; Higashiya Ginza is "a shop and teahouse that makes fine wagashi, the refined confectionery that accompanies green tea in Japan" (per Kinfolk feature on Higashiya Ginza).
- **Wagashi confections on a wooden or copper tray.** Higashiya's product line is the genre's referent (per Wallpaper* interview with Ogata: "I had to create a new way of enjoying tea").
- **Hand-forged iron kettle (tetsubin).** Functional and visual.
- **Stoneware tea caddy (chazutsu).** Lacquered or copper-cylinder, set on a wood shelf.
- **Wooden bench (engawa) or low stool.** Used as both furniture and as a staging surface for vignette photography.
- **Small water-basin (tsukubai) at the threshold.** Common Sukiya entry detail (per ArchDaily on Tokiwa).
- **Folded white linen cloth (chakin) on the counter.** Small, deliberate textile note.

## Reference projects

1. **SunnyHills at Minami-Aoyama, Tokyo — Kengo Kuma & Associates, completed 2013.** Three-storey retail and cafe (~293 m² total floor area; 102 m² building area; 175 m² site) for a Taiwanese pineapple-cake brand. Jigoku-gumi cypress lattice façade and ceiling; cork floor; complimentary pineapple cake and tea served on the 2F cafe. "Our aim was to create a forest in the busy city centre" — Kengo Kuma (per Dezeen 25 February 2014; Designboom 27 February 2014; Kengo Kuma & Associates project page; Interior Design magazine, *From A Simpler Time: SunnyHills by Kengo Kuma*).
2. **Higashiya Ginza, Tokyo — Shinichiro Ogata / Simplicity, opened 2003 brand line; Ginza location featured by Kinfolk.** Wagashi-and-teahouse hybrid; refined modern interpretation of traditional Japanese confectionery (per Kinfolk feature on Higashiya Ginza; Wallpaper* interview with Ogata).
3. **HIGASHIYA man Marunouchi, Tokyo — Shinichiro Ogata / Simplicity.** Latest addition to the HIGASHIYA family; "minimalist interior, composed of natural materials like whitewood and copper"; exclusive tea counter with "dozens of teas on display and a petite sabo, Japanese tea salon, specializing in the craft of how to enjoy different teas" (per Thisispaper).
4. **Yakumo Saryo, Tokyo — Shinichiro Ogata / Simplicity.** Japanese dining club and tea house in the studio's Saboe-tea tradition; part of the same Simplicity ecosystem as HIGASHIYA and the S[es] tableware line (per La Muse Blue 2018, *Shinichiro Ogata: Japanese design culture of SIMPLICITY and SABOE Japanese tea*).
5. **ori HIGASHIYA, Nishi-Azabu, Tokyo — Shinichiro Ogata / Simplicity.** Wagashi shop with a "Sabo tea room" featuring "traditional Japanese wrapping in a modern style of ori (folds)" (per La Muse Blue 2018).
6. **Hishiya guesthouse and restaurant, Kyoto — Fumihiko Sano Studio, completed 2021.** Renovation of vacant *minka* (folk house) into restaurant and four guest rooms; explicitly framed as a counter to "the dissipation of culture" caused by demolition of vacant historic homes (per Dezeen 19 November 2021, *Fumihiko Sano Studio converts Japanese minka into guesthouse and restaurant*).
7. **Japanese Cuisine Tokiwa, Tokyo — Fumihiko Sano Studio.** "The interior design is a Sukiya style to reflect the chef's personal fondness of tea ceremonies. The entire area is divided into an entrance, a private room, a counter, a table area" (per ArchDaily, *Japanese Cuisine Tokiwa / Fumihiko Sano Studio*).

## Common mistakes

1. **Treating "Japanese" as a Pinterest aesthetic of cherry blossoms + red lanterns.** The genre is rooted in Sukiya carpentry, wabi-sabi material restraint, and the wagashi-tea ritual (per ArchDaily Tokiwa; Wallpaper* on Ogata; Kinfolk on Higashiya Ginza). Cherry-blossom motifs and oversized red paper lanterns read as theme park, not cafe. Fix: replace with a single dried-branch ikebana and a single warm paper lantern.
2. **Conflating Japanese with Chinese tea-house vocabulary.** Carved dragons, deep-red lacquer columns, brocade cushions, gold-leaf wall panels are Chinese tea-house cues, not Japanese. The Japanese genre's accents are matcha green, sumi-ink black, copper, and natural cypress (per La Muse Blue 2018 on Saboe; Dezeen 2014 on SunnyHills lattice).
3. **Walnut as primary wood.** The genre's wood is hinoki, sugi cedar, cypress — pale and grain-prominent. SunnyHills uses 6×6 cm hinoki laths; HIGASHIYA Marunouchi uses "whitewood" with copper (per Dezeen 25 February 2014; Thisispaper). A walnut-paneled "tea cafe" reads as Western mid-century with Japanese props.
4. **Cool-white light flattening the patina.** Wabi-sabi explicitly values the "rusting patina of beauty" that develops over time (per La Muse Blue 2018 on Ogata) — that patina only reads under warm 2400–2700K light. Fix: clamp Kelvin to ≤2700K; never go above 3000K for a Japanese tea-cafe scene.
5. **Empty unstaged shelves.** Counter to insta-cafe (where the empty shelf is part of the look), the Japanese tea-cafe genre stages each shelf with one or two objects: matcha bowl, tea caddy, single-stem vase. Higashiya Marunouchi has an "exclusive tea counter with dozens of teas on display" — display is curated, not bare (per Thisispaper).
6. **Skipping the lattice / shoji light-filtering effect.** SunnyHills' published photography is dominated by the slat-shadow pattern across cork (per Designboom 27 February 2014). A render of a "Japanese tea cafe" with flat ambient and no lattice shadow loses the genre's defining light texture. Fix: add a wood-slat lattice or shoji partition between sun and floor; let the sun pattern the room.
