# Style: Scandinavian (Skandinavisk)

> Sources: Charlotte & Peter Fiell, *Scandinavian Design* (Taschen, 40th Anniversary ed., 2022); Meik Wiking, *The Little Book of Hygge: The Danish Way to Live Well* (Penguin, 2016); Dezeen feature articles (2017-2023); Wallpaper* features on Vipp; Architectural Record feature on Note Design Studio. Specific named projects cited inline.
> Last updated: 2026-04-29

## Anchor description

Scandinavian design is the marriage of organic form with everyday functionality, born of a Nordic "democratic design" tradition that bridges craft and industrial production (per Fiell, *Scandinavian Design*, intro essay). At the residential scale it expresses itself as **hygge** — a cultivated atmosphere of comfort, togetherness, and well-being created mainly through tactile natural materials and warm low-Kelvin light, prioritizing "rustic over new, simple over posh, ambience over excitement" (per Wiking, *The Little Book of Hygge*, 2016, ch. 2). For a 3D scene this means: light wood floors, off-white walls, soft wool textiles, a single dark or muted accent, and many small warm light sources rather than one bright ceiling fixture.

Practical rule for an AI applying this palette: pick **one** dominant (white or warm grey, not both equally), **one** secondary wood tone (pale oak), and **one** accent — soft black, oxidized brass, sage, or chocolate. Mixing two strong accents breaks the calm.

## Material vocabulary

### In

- **Pale-grain hardwoods — oak, ash, birch.** Scandinavian design is rooted in native timber craft and specifically in the post-war embrace of organic form in wood (per Fiell, *Scandinavian Design*, on Norway/Sweden/Denmark/Finland/Iceland furniture traditions). The Vipp Loft makes the canonical move: wide Dinesen oak plank flooring + exposed oak rafters as the room's structural texture (per Remodelista feature on Vipp Loft).
- **Wool, linen, boucle, sheepskin throws.** Hygge prioritizes "things made of wood … blankets and cushions … tactile stuff" and clothing/textiles "rely on soft natural fabrics like wool" (per Wiking, *Little Book of Hygge*, ch. 6 "Home").
- **Vegetable-tanned / unfinished leather.** Used for sling chairs and strap details on cabinetry; reads as ageable rather than polished (per Fiell on Danish modern furniture's "organic forms and functionality").
- **Matte ceramic, low-saturation glaze.** Hygge home checklist explicitly includes "Ceramics, tactile stuff, vintage stuff" (per Wiking, ch. 6).
- **Brass — oxidized / brushed, never bright.** The Audo uses "warming brass accents to lift the industrial architecture" — warmed and dulled, not mirror-polished (per Dezeen 2019).
- **Lime-washed brick, raw concrete (industrial-Scandi crossover).** Vipp Chimney House: "lime-washed brickwork and terrazzo pebble floors" inside an old brick frame (per Wallpaper*/Dwell coverage of Vipp Chimney House by Studio David Thulstrup).

### Out

- **High-gloss anything, chrome, mirror polish.** Wiking is explicit: "the more bling, the less hygge" (per Wiking, ch. 2). Bright chrome reads anti-Scandinavian for the same reason cool fluorescent light does.
- **Veiny showy marble as a hero surface.** The Audo uses "veiny marble coffee tables" only as small punctuation against boucle and concrete; marble is never the dominant 60% in a Scandinavian scheme (per Dezeen 2019). Treat it as 10% accent at most.
- **Ornate carving, gilt, baroque mouldings.** Scandinavian design's defining move was the *rejection* of mid-century glamour and ornamentation in favor of democratic, mass-producible organic form (per Fiell, intro essay on the post-war "democratic design" thesis).
- **Cool-white fluorescent / 5000K+ tubes.** Wiking's signature image: "the closest you will ever come to seeing vampires burnt by daylight is by inviting a group of Danes for a hygge dinner and then placing them under a 5,000K fluorescent light tube" (per Wiking, ch. 1 "Light").

## Lighting profile

- **Kelvin range: 2200-2700K.** Wiking's "hygge sweet spot" sits around candle/sunset color, ~1800K, and explicitly states "the lower the temperature of the light is, the more hygge" (per Wiking, ch. 1). For practical 3D rendering use 2200-2400K for candle-mimicking accent lights and 2700K as the warm upper bound for general LED fixtures. Cross-reference `lighting.md` for the project-wide Kelvin baseline; Scandinavian sits at the **warm end** of the project-wide range.
- **Layer mix: many small sources, no single big ceiling lamp.** "Usually, several smaller lamps around the room create a more hyggeligt light than one big lamp set in the ceiling. You want to create small caves of light around the room" (per Wiking, ch. 1). Translate to: ambient at 50-80 lx, plus 4-8 per-table / per-corner accents at 20-40 lx each, plus candles/decorative.
- **Decorative lighting is non-optional.** "Danes spend a disproportionate amount of their income on lighting and some of their most popular designers are designers of lamps" (per Wiking, ch. 1). A Scandinavian render without a hero pendant or floor lamp reads incomplete.
- **Fixture style — the canonical references:**
  - **Louis Poulsen PH 5 pendant** (Poul Henningsen, 1958) — three-shade glare-free pyramid; "can still be found in almost every other home in Denmark" (per Louis Poulsen heritage page on the PH 5).
  - **Louis Poulsen PH Artichoke** (1958) — for taller volumes / lobby / above-stair.
  - **Vipp pendant + wall fixtures** as paired with Studio David Thulstrup interiors (Vipp Loft, Vipp Chimney House — per Wallpaper*).
  - **Paper / linen drum shades, simple cylindrical pendants** as the budget vernacular extension of the Henningsen tradition.
- **Candles always.** "Denmark burns two times as many candles per person than anywhere else in Europe" — candles rank #2 on the Danish things-associated-with-happiness list, just behind hot drinks (per Wiking, ch. 1). Place tapered or pillar candles on dining tables, coffee tables, and shelf vignettes by default for Scandinavian hero shots.

## Camera bias

- **Wide soft daylight, often through a single tall window.** The recurring framing convention in Norm Architects' published interiors (The Audo and the studio's residential portfolio on Dezeen) is a 24-35mm equivalent lens looking *into* a corner where wall + window + furniture stack, with daylight as the diffuse key (per Dezeen tag/Norm Architects coverage; The Audo reportage 2019).
- **Eye height 1.4-1.6m** for residential vignettes (the seated-conversation height) — emphasizes the low silhouettes of Scandinavian furniture (per general residential photography convention as visible in Wallpaper* Vipp Loft and Remodelista feature framing).
- **Vertical correction on.** Architectural Scandinavian publications uniformly straighten verticals; tilted-up amateur framing instantly breaks the editorial read (per ASMP / Norman McGrath conventions referenced project-wide; cross-ref `camera.md`).
- **Foreground prop, midground furniture group, background daylight wall.** This three-layer depth is the published Norm/Vipp tell — boucle sofa or oak bench in foreground, dining/lounge cluster mid, white wall + window behind (per Dezeen 2019 The Audo image set; Wallpaper* Vipp Loft image set).

## Prop vocabulary

Hygge home checklist, applied directly as a 3D prop checklist (per Wiking, ch. 6 "Home" — "items that make a home more hyggeligt: a nook to curl up in, a fireplace, candles, things made of wood, nature, books, ceramics, tactile stuff, vintage stuff, blankets and cushions"):

- **Plants — large monstera, olive tree, rubber fig, or a single tall dried branch.** "Nature" is on Wiking's hygge home list; one tall planted specimen reads more Scandinavian than three small succulents.
- **Books — coffee-table format, stacked horizontally.** Books are explicitly on the hygge home list (per Wiking, ch. 6); a stack of Taschen / Phaidon / Kinfolk-format hardcovers on the coffee table is the Scandinavian editorial tell.
- **Ceramic vessels — matte, low saturation, hand-thrown silhouette.** Per the Wiking home list; pair an off-white stoneware vase with a single dried branch.
- **Wool throws and linen cushions.** "Blankets and cushions" on the hygge home list (per Wiking, ch. 6); always 1-2 visibly draped, not folded military-tight.
- **Candles — tapered (dining), pillar (coffee table), tealights (shelf).** See Lighting Profile above.
- **One vintage piece per room.** "Vintage stuff" on the hygge home list (per Wiking, ch. 6) — a mid-century PK22 chair, an old Wegner Wishbone, or a worn leather strap-handled cabinet. The Vipp Loft places "Poul Kjaerholm PK22 Chairs" beside the green velvet sofa as exactly this move (per Remodelista feature on Vipp Loft).

## Reference projects

1. **Norm Architects + MENU, *The Audo* (now Audo House), Copenhagen — Dezeen, 4 June 2019** ("The Audo hotel by Menu and Norm Architects doubles as a showroom"). 1918 industrial Nordhavn building; concrete tile floor, perforated black metal ceiling, white boucle sofas, veiny marble coffee tables, chocolate velvet armchairs, brass accents.
2. **Studio David Thulstrup, *Vipp Loft*, Copenhagen — Wallpaper*, 2017** ("One-room wonders: Danish brand Vipp launches its new hotel line"). Dinesen oak flooring, exposed oak rafters, green velvet sofa, Poul Kjaerholm PK22 chairs, Aiayu linens.
3. **Studio David Thulstrup, *Vipp Chimney House*, Copenhagen — Wallpaper* / Dwell, 2020** ("Vipp Chimney House opens in Copenhagen"). Lime-washed brickwork, terrazzo pebble floors, monolithic concrete box inside the old chimney shell.
4. **Julie Cloos Mølsgaard, *Vipp Pencil Case*, Copenhagen — Dezeen, 22 March 2022** ("Vipp sets up one-room hotel inside ex-pencil factory in Copenhagen") and Wallpaper* coverage. Bauhaus-era 1930s pencil factory; airy white walls, beige-to-warm-grey neutral palette, oak dining table with Jura stone counter, powder-grey V1 kitchen, Kvadrat floor-to-ceiling curtains.
5. **Note Design Studio, *Hidden Tints*, Stockholm — Architectural Record, Sept 2017; Dezeen 31 May 2017; Dezeen Awards 2018 Residential Interior of the Year**. Counter-example useful as a *boundary case*: shows a more colorful Stockholm Scandinavian (mustard yellow, sage green, dusty pink, salmon) sourced from the building's own 19th-century paint history. Cite when a user wants Scandinavian *with color* rather than the all-neutral Copenhagen mode.
6. **Poul Henningsen for Louis Poulsen, *PH 5 pendant lamp* (1958)** — fixture-level reference; the canonical Scandinavian ceiling pendant per Louis Poulsen heritage materials and 1stDibs Introspective 150th-anniversary feature.

## Common mistakes

1. **All-white-no-warmth — "the IKEA showroom mistake."** Defaulting every surface to pure white plus cool LED produces a sterile catalog look that Scandinavian publications explicitly distance themselves from. The Vipp Pencil Case wins because the white is paired with **beige-to-warm-grey** neutrals and a powder-grey kitchen, not bare white (per Wallpaper* feature on Vipp Pencil Case). Fix: introduce the warm grey 30% and at least one accent before rendering.
2. **One bright ceiling lamp instead of many small caves of light.** Wiking explicitly names this as the anti-hygge mistake — "several smaller lamps around the room create a more hyggeligt light than one big lamp set in the ceiling" (per Wiking, ch. 1). Fix: scatter 4-8 warm 2200-2700K accents at table / corner / shelf height; treat ceiling fixture as decorative, not as the primary lux source.
3. **Mass-market "Scandi" props — fake reindeer hides, plastic-handle copper pots, neon "hygge" signs.** These are catalog tropes, not Scandinavian design. Fiell anchors actual Scandinavian design in 125+ named designers and the democratic-design tradition (per Fiell, *Scandinavian Design*, on the 125-designer scope) — a scene built from named-designer vintage (PK22, Wishbone, PH 5) reads authentic; a scene built from "Scandi style" airport-shop tat does not.
4. **Cool-white light in any quantity.** Wiking ridicules the 5000K fluorescent dinner-table scenario as the literal opposite of hygge (per Wiking, ch. 1). Fix: clamp every light source to ≤2700K for Scandinavian scenes; if the user has chosen daylight ambience, render at golden hour (≈3000K + low sun angle), not noon-cool.
5. **Treating marble as a hero surface.** Marble appears in named Scandinavian projects (The Audo coffee tables) but always as small punctuation — never as the dominant kitchen counter or floor (per Dezeen 2019 The Audo feature). Defaulting to a marble island reads more "luxury developer apartment" than Scandinavian.
