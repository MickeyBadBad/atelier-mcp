# Style: Speakeasy / Vintage Bar

> Sources: Wikipedia, "Please Don't Tell" (PDT) entry citing Jim Meehan and the bar's 2007 opening; Apothéke Mixology official site and 6sqft 2022 coverage of Apotheke NoMad; Hospitality Design 2022 feature on Apotheke NoMad; Yellowtrace / Roman and Williams documentation of Le Coucou (11 Howard, NYC); Roman and Williams official page for Chapel Bar NYC; The World's 50 Best Bars discovery page on Death & Co; SCMP 2018 feature on PDT Hong Kong by NC Design and Architecture; Architectural Digest "best speakeasies" coverage referenced by Apotheke press.
> Last updated: 2026-04-29

## Anchor description

The "speakeasy" is a recognized global hospitality genre that revives the visual and ritual language of US Prohibition-era (1920–1933) clandestine bars: a hidden or barely-marked entrance, low-lit interior, cocktail-craft bartenders in period dress, and a tightly-controlled atmosphere of intimacy and secrecy. The form was canonized commercially by Please Don't Tell (PDT), opened May 2007 in New York's East Village behind a working phone booth inside the Crif Dogs hot-dog shop, "often cited as the first speakeasy-style bar and thus originator of the modern speakeasy trend" (per Wikipedia, *Please Don't Tell*, citing Jim Meehan and co-founders Brian Shebairo and Chris Antista). For a 3D scene this means: dark stained-wood paneling, pressed-tin or coffered ceilings, exposed brick, marble or brass bar tops, deep velvet upholstery, dim candlelit pools at 1800–2400K, and a single hidden or "barely-marked" door rather than a glowing storefront (per Wikipedia/PDT and Yelp/Death & Co coverage of the "barely-marked wooden door" entrance).

Practical rule for an AI applying this palette: pick **one** dominant (dark wood panel **or** exposed brick — not both at full saturation), **one** rich textile (oxblood, burgundy, or sage velvet), and **one** metal (brass, copper, or gold leaf). Mixing two strong textiles plus three metals reads "steakhouse," not speakeasy.

## Material vocabulary

### In

- **Dark stained hardwoods — walnut, mahogany, dark oak panel.** The PDT signature interior is described as a "wood-paneled den" with a "pressed-tin ceiling and the vintage rotary phone" (per New Yorks City Secrets feature on PDT). Use as wainscoting up to 1.4 m or as full-height floor-to-ceiling panel.
- **Exposed red brick.** Death & Co's defining surface — "exposed brick, low-slung tables and a marble bar top" (per The World's 50 Best Bars, *Death & Co* discovery page). Brick should read aged, with mortar visibly recessed; new-looking brick breaks the era.
- **Pressed-tin ceiling tiles.** PDT's "pressed-tin ceiling" is part of the canonical speakeasy reading (per New Yorks City Secrets PDT feature). Where pressed tin is unavailable, a dark coffered ceiling with deep shadow lines is the substitute.
- **Velvet — oxblood, burgundy, deep green, dusty rose.** Roman and Williams' Chapel Bar is described as "dreamy interiors of soft lighting and velvet furniture" (per Roman and Williams, *Chapel Bar* project page); Apotheke NoMad uses sage-green velvet curtains at the entrance threshold (per Hospitality Design 2022).
- **Brass and aged copper, never bright chrome.** Le Coucou (Roman and Williams, 11 Howard) puts the bar logic plainly: "a simple French marble counter atop a dark panelled, timber cabinet reads more like a beautiful piece of furniture" — restraint in metal so the wood and marble can carry the room (per Yellowtrace 2016 feature on Le Coucou).
- **Marble bar tops — figured, never bright white quartz.** Death & Co's "marble bar top" and Apotheke NoMad's "20-foot rosa aurora marble bar" are the canonical move (per The World's 50 Best Bars; per Hospitality Design 2022).
- **Reclaimed barnwood floors, vintage ceramic tile inlays.** Apotheke NoMad uses "reclaimed barnwood floors inlaid with vintage ceramic tile" — also chosen for sound absorption alongside custom upholstery (per 6sqft 2022, Apotheke NoMad).
- **Period taxidermy, antique apothecary glassware, vintage rotary phones.** PDT is "adorned with taxidermy and vintage decor" and prominently features a vintage rotary phone (per Wikipedia *Please Don't Tell*; New Yorks City Secrets).

### Out

- **Cool-white LED, exposed Edison-style filament if used at >2700K.** The genre's defining atmosphere is "dim" and "warmly lit" (per Wikipedia *PDT*; per Hospitality Design 2022 on Apotheke NoMad). Cool light kills the mood and reads as "industrial loft," not speakeasy.
- **Bright signage, oversized logos, glowing storefronts.** Death & Co's street entrance is "in plain sight, but it's a barely-marked wooden door with the name of the establishment written in such small print that you could easily miss it" (per Yelp / Death & Co, NYC). PDT's "only clue" is the phone booth inside the hot-dog shop (per Wikipedia *PDT*); Apotheke Doyers has "only a small sign that says 'Chemist'" (per Chowhound 2024 feature on Apotheke).
- **High-gloss white stone, polished chrome bar fronts.** These read "modernist hotel lobby," not speakeasy. Le Coucou's bar by Roman and Williams reads as a piece of furniture, not a luxury slab (per Yellowtrace 2016).
- **Loud, bright, large-volume rooms.** PDT seats "no more than four dozen people" (per Wikipedia *PDT*). Density and intimacy are part of the genre — sparse big rooms read as restaurant, not speakeasy.

## Lighting profile

- **Kelvin range: 1800–2400K.** Apotheke NoMad's signature room is described as "warmly lit" with sage curtains parting to reveal it (per Hospitality Design 2022); Chapel Bar is defined by "soft lighting" against velvet (per Roman and Williams). Cross-reference `lighting.md` for the project-wide warm-end clamp; speakeasy sits at the very warm extreme — colder than 2700K reads "loft," not "Prohibition."
- **Layer mix: heavy accent + decorative, minimal ambient.** Translate to ambient at 30–60 lx, table-level accents at 80–150 lx (just enough to read a menu and see the cocktail's color), and decorative (sconces, candles, beaker-lamps) at 20–40 lx. Apotheke's original Doyers room has "lamps … colorful beakers of mysterious infusions" — the lamp itself is decor (per Chowhound 2024 / Apothéke Mixology).
- **Candles always on.** PDT, Death & Co, Apotheke, and Chapel Bar all rely on candle pools for table-level light. Render at least one tealight or pillar candle per occupied 2-top.
- **Hidden / theatrical entrance lighting.** PDT's phone booth, Apotheke Doyers' "Chemist" sign, Death & Co's barely-marked door, and Apotheke NoMad's herb-and-wildflower descent staircase are part of the lighting choreography — the threshold drops the guest from street brightness to room dimness across 2–3 m of dark transition (per Wikipedia *PDT*; per 6sqft 2022).
- **Backbar as the bright object.** Apotheke NoMad's "hand-carved back bar displays a colorful array of infused spirits, fresh herbal tonics, and house-made bitters" — the backbar is the lit hero, not the ceiling (per Hospitality Design 2022).

## Camera bias

- **Tight 35–50mm equivalent, eye-height seated (1.1–1.3 m).** Speakeasy publication framing (Yellowtrace's Le Coucou set, Roman and Williams' Chapel Bar set) almost universally shoots from a barstool's perspective rather than a standing wide architectural shot — preserves the room's intimacy.
- **Foreground: candle / cocktail glass / bottle vignette.** Yellowtrace's Le Coucou reportage features the bar's "arched joinery displaying the myriad of coloured liquids bottled in decanted crystal" as the foreground hero (per Yellowtrace 2016).
- **Backbar as light source frames the human silhouette.** Apotheke NoMad's "infinity"-mirrored ceiling channels combined with the backbar lighting are documented as the room's primary published angle (per Hospitality Design 2022 on Apotheke NoMad's mirrored channels).
- **Vertical correction on, but accept slight tilt for the threshold reveal.** The hidden-entrance shot — phone booth (PDT), "Chemist" sign (Apotheke Doyers), or sage-curtain descent (Apotheke NoMad) — is the genre's signature non-architectural angle.

## Prop vocabulary

- **Vintage rotary phone or candlestick phone.** PDT's phone booth is the canonical entry prop (per Wikipedia *PDT*).
- **Apothecary jars, beakers, copper stills.** Apotheke's mixologists "wear white lab coats" and order categories include "aphrodisiacs," "euphorics," and "health & beauty" — beakers and jars are core to the genre's "alchemical" reading (per Apothéke Mixology official site; Chowhound 2024).
- **Decanted crystal bottles, color-graded backbar.** Le Coucou's bar is read as "arched joinery displaying the myriad of coloured liquids bottled in decanted crystal" (per Yellowtrace 2016).
- **Period taxidermy or framed botanical prints.** PDT is "adorned with taxidermy and vintage decor" (per Wikipedia *PDT*); Apotheke uses dried herbs and wildflowers along the entry stair (per 6sqft 2022).
- **Velvet tufted Chesterfield sofa or banquette.** The genre's seating type — supports the deep upholstery / dim-light reading.
- **Stained-glass panels or mirrored channels at ceiling.** Apotheke NoMad's penthouse has "a floor-to-ceiling wall of custom stained-glass windows, each panel featuring the Apotheke name" and "channels in the speakeasy ceiling … clad with handprinted wallpaper and capped with mirrors to create an illusion of infinity" (per Hospitality Design 2022; 6sqft 2022).
- **Bartender uniform: bow tie, suspenders, pharmacist coat.** Death & Co bartenders are documented "in their bow ties and suspenders" (per The World's 50 Best Bars discovery page); Apotheke mixologists "play the part of pharmacists, stirring up novel and original libations while wearing white lab coats" (per Chowhound 2024 / Apothéke Mixology).

## Reference projects

1. **Please Don't Tell (PDT), New York — Jim Meehan / Brian Shebairo / Chris Antista, opened May 2007.** East Village, hidden behind a vintage phone booth inside Crif Dogs at 113 St. Marks Place; "often cited as the first speakeasy-style bar and thus originator of the modern speakeasy trend" (per Wikipedia, *Please Don't Tell*); Hong Kong outpost designed by Nelson Chow / NC Design and Architecture (per SCMP 2018).
2. **Death & Co, New York — David Kaplan and Ravi DeRossi, opened New Year's Eve 2006/07.** East Village; "exposed brick, low-slung tables and a marble bar top," bartenders "in their bow ties and suspenders," "barely-marked wooden door" entrance (per The World's 50 Best Bars, *Death & Co*; Yelp Death & Co listing). Won Best American Cocktail Bar at Tales of the Cocktail Spirited Awards 2010.
3. **Apothéke, New York Chinatown — Christopher Tierney, opened 2008** at 9 Doyers Street in a former opium den; "first of its kind … inspired by the history and rise of the apothecary in Europe as well as the artistic influence of absinthe dens in 19th-century Paris" (per Apothéke Mixology official site; Chowhound 2024). Recognized by Architectural Digest among best speakeasies in the US (per Apothéke Mixology press).
4. **Apotheke NoMad, New York — opened 2022, Christopher Tierney design.** 9 West 26th Street; sage-green velvet curtains, 20-foot rosa aurora marble bar, reclaimed barnwood + vintage ceramic tile floor, mirrored ceiling channels with handprinted wallpaper, stained-glass penthouse (per Hospitality Design 2022; 6sqft 2022). Won SBID's "Best Designed Bar in America" 2023 (per LOOP Design Awards / Apotheke NoMad project page).
5. **Le Coucou, New York — Roman and Williams (Robin Standefer & Stephen Alesch), opened 2016** inside 11 Howard, SoHo. Whitewashed brick walls, pale oak floors, exposed raw concrete ceiling, French marble bar atop dark panelled timber cabinet, hand-painted mural by Dean Barger (per Yellowtrace 2016 feature on Le Coucou). Roman and Williams have topped Architectural Digest's AD 100 list eight times since 2012 (per Yellowtrace 2016 / AD coverage).
6. **Chapel Bar, New York — Roman and Williams.** Mid-19th-century chapel-turned-bar with "soft lighting and velvet furniture, alongside perfectly balanced cocktails and polished hospitality" (per Roman and Williams, *Chapel Bar* project page).

## Common mistakes

1. **Too-bright Edison filaments at high Kelvin / high lux.** "Speakeasy" rendered with 4000K filament bulbs at 200+ lx ambient reads "industrial brewery," not Prohibition. Apotheke NoMad and PDT both rely on warm pools at 1800–2400K with sub-60 lx ambient (per Hospitality Design 2022; Wikipedia *PDT* "dimly lit"). Fix: clamp ambient to ≤60 lx, all sources to ≤2400K.
2. **Glowing storefront / oversized logo.** Death & Co is famous for its "barely-marked wooden door"; PDT's only clue is a phone booth inside a hot-dog shop; Apotheke Doyers' only signage is a small "Chemist" plate (per Yelp Death & Co; Wikipedia *PDT*; Chowhound 2024). A bright LED-channel sign over the door breaks the genre.
3. **Stacking velvet, brass, marble, and stained glass all at full strength.** Apotheke NoMad gets away with maximalism because it stages each material in a different room (subterranean speakeasy = barnwood + marble; penthouse = dusty rose + gold leaf + stained glass — per 6sqft 2022). In a single 3D scene, pick one rich textile + one metal + one stone; layering all three reads as a Vegas pastiche.
4. **Forgetting the threshold transition.** The hidden entrance is part of the rendered narrative for any hero shot of the genre — the dim 2–3 m approach (PDT phone booth, Apotheke sage-curtain stair, Death & Co barely-marked door) sets the eye for the dim room. Skipping it produces a plain dark bar.
5. **Friends-IP / TV-show theming as substitute for genre.** The speakeasy genre is rooted in Prohibition history (per Wikipedia *PDT* on cocktail-revival lineage; Apotheke NoMad's "ancient future" apothecary concept per Hospitality Design 2022). Layering pop-culture posters or sitcom imagery on top reads as theme bar, not speakeasy. Lean on period objects (rotary phone, taxidermy, apothecary glass, pressed tin) instead.
